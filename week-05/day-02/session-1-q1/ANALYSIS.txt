English-to-German Sequence-to-Sequence Translation

The experiment used 29,000 Multi30k training pairs, 1,014 validation pairs, and
1,000 held-out test pairs. Separate English and German tokenizers were fitted
only on training sentences, preserving punctuation and providing an unknown-word
token. Text was lowercased. English inputs were post-padded or truncated to
20 tokens. German sequences reserved positions for start and end markers,
leaving at most 18 content tokens before shifting for teacher forcing.

The encoder and decoder each used 256-dimensional embeddings and a 512-unit
LSTM. Encoder hidden and cell states initialized the decoder. A softmax layer
predicted the next German token across the training vocabulary. Adam minimized
sparse categorical cross-entropy over ten epochs with batches of 64. Padding
was masked and excluded from token accuracy. The model had 25,573,662 trainable
parameters. Tokenizers and separate inference models were saved for reuse.

Training loss decreased from 5.5127 to 1.7248, while validation loss decreased
from 4.7368 to 3.4903. Training token accuracy rose from 20.99% to 61.53%;
validation token accuracy rose from 25.65% to 46.23%. The final accuracy gap
was 15.30 percentage points. Validation loss improved in every epoch, but
improvements became small near the end. This suggests diminishing generalization
gains and increasing specialization to training examples, rather than a clear
validation-loss reversal. Held-out test loss was 3.3834 and token accuracy was
47.20%, broadly consistent with validation performance.

These accuracies use teacher forcing: each prediction receives the correct
preceding German tokens. They do not measure complete-sentence translation
accuracy. Greedy decoding instead feeds predictions back into the decoder,
allowing early mistakes to propagate. The saved examples demonstrate this
limitation. A caption about repairing a roof became a description of taking
a photograph of a building. Another prediction changed a Boston Terrier into
a beagle and introduced a beach and ball. The snow scene retained people and
helmets but repeated words and omitted important details.

The system therefore demonstrates the required translation pipeline, but its
outputs are not reliably faithful or grammatically correct. A single fixed-size
context and short sequence limit constrain information retention. Future
experiments could compare attention, subword tokenization, regularization, and
beam search. Evaluation should include corpus-level translation metrics and
human checks for meaning preservation. Those extensions were not included in
this fixed-architecture run, and no deployment-quality claim follows from the
reported token accuracy.
